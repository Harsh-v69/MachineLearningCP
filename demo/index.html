Solver outcome

All three graphs are built from the same two candidates (one optimal, one adversarial). The adversarial one never reaches the goal in any of them, so the solver picks the optimal path every time; none of the three extensions change whether this episode succeeds. What they do change is the price tag attached to trusting the path. Phase 2 discards the dead branch instead of leaving it in the graph unused. Phase 4 prices every edge by regression (did this step move a box farther from its goal), confidence, and budget pressure together, not confidence alone, then scales everything by 10x to keep one decimal of precision as a whole number for the solver. Phase 3 pushes the cost higher again: one earlier episode already recorded a mismatch on a specific step, so that exact transition is trusted less here than it was under plain Phase 2, even though the validator's own verdict on it hasn't changed.

Does this matter at scale?

On the goal-biased mock LLM, corner mistakes like the one above almost never happen by chance, so the aggregate metrics look identical with or without the validator. To find out whether it actually matters, we stress-tested it with a deliberately much dumber candidate generator (pure random legal moves, no safety-net optimal plan) across 30 trials on a harder two-box level, then independently re-checked every rejection with a plain breadth-first search that has nothing to do with the validator's own logic.
